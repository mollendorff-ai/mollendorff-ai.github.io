A competitor cut prices: should I match?

Match if more than a quarter of your customers would leave if you held your price. That share is the cut divided by what you earn on a sale (2 ÷ 8). Below it, holding earns more. Above a third, a price war is certain, and both shops end up 2,000 a month poorer.

The street

Two shops sell the same thing for 20, which costs each of them 12, and each sells 1,000 a month. Your rival cuts its price by a tenth, to 18, so a sale leaves 6 instead of 8. If you hold your price, some of your customers go to the cheaper shop. By this plan, 28% of them.

Matching earns 6,000 a month. Holding earns 5,760 (you keep 72% of the customers, at the full margin). So at 28%, matching earns 240 a month more, a small gain, and the street has two stable outcomes: both hold at 8,000 each, or both cut at 6,000 each. Which one happens depends on what each shop expects of the other.

One number decides

Matching pays when the share of customers who would leave is more than the cut divided by your margin, 2 ÷ 8, a quarter. Three zones follow:

  • Below a quarter: holding is the only stable outcome. The rival’s cut was a mistake.
  • Between a quarter and a third: both outcomes are stable, and the street can go either way.
  • Above a third (2 ÷ 6): cutting pays whatever the other does, so both cut and each earns 2,000 a month less than if both had held. That is the price war.

Tap fickle_customers (45 in 100 would leave) or loyal_customers (15 in 100) in the model to see the street move from one zone to another.

How sure is the answer?

Sure of the thresholds, not of where your customers are. Nobody knows how many would leave. If it is anywhere between 10% and 50%, most likely 28%, then over 5,000 simulated futures matching pays in 66 of 100, being the first to cut would pay in 28, and the street has two stable outcomes in 38.

The first thing to find out is how many of your customers shop on price alone. The uncertain version of the model is price_cut_unsure.yaml; run forge simulate on it.

What it assumes

  • The two shops are the same: the same costs, the same prices, the same customers.
  • Customers who leave go to the cheaper shop, and none come back.
  • The game is played once, not month after month; repeated play can make holding stable even above a third (Forge’s examples have the long game).
