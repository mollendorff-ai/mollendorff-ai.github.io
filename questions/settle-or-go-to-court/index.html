Accept the settlement offer or go to court?

Take the 40,000. A trial alone would be worth 48,000 on average, which is more than the offer, but you spend 15,000 before you get there, and refusing is worth only 33,800. You should refuse only if you are more than 65.4 in 100 sure to win, and this claim is 60.

The offer

The other side offers 40,000 today. Your claim is for 120,000, and your lawyer thinks you win 6 times in 10. Refusing costs 15,000 of the lawyer’s work before the trial. In 4 cases in 10 the other side then offers 50,000, which you take. Otherwise you go to trial, which costs 20,000 more, and if you lose you pay 10,000 of the other side’s costs.

What refusing really means

Refusing is not one outcome but three, and the model gives all of them. In 36 cases in 100 you win the trial and end 85,000 ahead. In 40 you get the better offer and end 35,000 ahead (the 50,000, less the 15,000 already spent). In 24 you lose the trial and end 45,000 behind. The average is 33,800: less than the 40,000 you would have had for certain.

The turning point is a chance of winning of 65.4 in 100 (85 ÷ 130). Above it, going to trial is worth 55,000 or more after its own costs, and refusing beats the offer. Below it, the offer wins.

How sure is the answer?

As sure as your chance of winning, and that is the one number nobody knows. A lawyer’s 6 in 10 is a judgment, not a measurement. At 60 the offer wins by 6,200; at 66 refusing wins narrowly. So the useful question is how far above or below 65 in 100 your case really is, and what you would pay to be sure.

What it assumes

  • Every amount is what the claimant receives or pays; interest, tax and the time a trial takes are left out.
  • The other side’s better offer, if there is one, comes after you have spent the 15,000 and is final.
  • The chances are a lawyer’s guesses. Costs and who pays them differ with the court and the province: this is a model of the arithmetic, not legal advice.

All figures are made up, for illustration. This is not financial, legal or tax advice.
