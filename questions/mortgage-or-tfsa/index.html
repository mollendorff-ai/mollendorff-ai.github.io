Pay down the mortgage or put the money in a TFSA?

At a return of 6% a year the TFSA ends 2,793 ahead of paying down a 4.79% mortgage over ten years, which is little for the risk. The fund wins in 59 futures out of 100 and loses in 41, and one time in four it ends more than 5,000 behind.

The two ways to use 300 a month

You have 300 a month for ten years, 36,000 in all. Paid on the mortgage, it lowers what you owe, for certain: the loan is 4.79% fixed, and in Canada fixed rates compound twice a year, so the true yearly cost is 4.85%. Put in an index fund in a TFSA, it earns a return nobody knows, with no tax on the gains. Mortgage interest on a home is not deductible, so there is no tax on that side either.

The model takes the fund’s average yearly return over the ten years as 6%, give or take 5 points: usually between 1% and 11%.

How sure is the answer?

Sure that the choice is a bet, not sure who wins it. At a return of exactly 6% the TFSA ends with 48,742 and the mortgage is 45,949 lower, so the TFSA is ahead by 2,793. Spread the return over 10,000 futures and the gap goes from about 10,700 behind in a bad tenth of them (−10,665) to 22,100 ahead in a good tenth.

The mortgage’s side never moves: it is the return of a risk-free investment at 4.85%. The more the fund’s average return is above that, the more it wins, and the model says nothing about what returns to expect. That is the part you must supply.

What it assumes

  • The mortgage rate stays 4.79% for the ten years. In practice a term renews every five at the rate of the day.
  • The return is the fund’s average over the ten years, after fees, and the TFSA has room for the money.
  • Nothing is kept aside for an emergency: money paid on a mortgage cannot be taken back, and money in a TFSA can.

All figures are made up, for illustration. This is not financial, legal or tax advice.
